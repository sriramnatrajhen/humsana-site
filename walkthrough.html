Five steps from your own command to a signed record.

You define an action class of your own and send it to the live service. You change one field and read the refusal, with the field, both values and the rule. You download the signed record for that press and check it offline. You change one word of the record and watch the check fail. You run an executor that asks before it acts, and read the count that says its rail ran once.

No account, no key and nothing installed: the client and the verifier are the standard library only.

Open the sandbox Ask for the two files

Every command on this page was run on 28 September 2026 against https://api.humsana.com, from one directory holding a copy of the boundary client and the offline verifier and nothing else. Every line of output under a step is that run, copied unedited. Step 1, step 2 and step 5 need the boundary client, which is standard library only. Step 3 and step 4 need the verifier and the two files it checks: the record and the public key, both downloaded from the sandbox. The client and the verifier are handed to an integrator today rather than published for download, so if you want them in your own hands, ask.

Step 01your own action class, anonymously

Drive your action through the live service.

The action class is a name you choose. In the run below it is yourteam.release, with three parameters bound to it: an amount, an account and a reference.

The client sends one call to POST /v1/authorize/verify and reads the answer back. No key, no account and no signup: the public service accepts anonymous calls, rate limited per caller.

Step 03the record, and the check

Download the record for that press and check it offline.

The sandbox issues a signed record for the action it presented. The file is served beside the page at /sandbox/gregg/record.receipt.json, and the public key beside it at /sandbox/gregg/key.pub.json.

The record route answers only in a session that has pressed, so the calls before the download carry the cookie the sandbox set, kept in jar.txt by curl. The press below changes the same field as step 2, the account from 4821 to 9137. It answers with a different code, GRANT_DOES_NOT_COVER_THIS_ACTION rather than EFFECTUATION_DRIFT, for a reason worth knowing: this press presents an action the grant in this session does not cover, and the service answers that the grant does not cover it rather than comparing field by field. The same change, sent against a binding the caller established itself, is the comparison that names the field and the rule, which is what step 2 shows.

$ curl -s -c jar.txt -o /dev/null -w 'GET  %{url_effective}  %{http_code}\n' https://api.humsana.com/sandbox/gregg/

Step 04one word changed in the record

Change one word of the record and watch the check fail.

The record is signed over its canonical body, so every value in it is covered by the signature except the record's own digest field, which is the hash of exactly the bytes that were signed. Change the verdict from REFUSED to ALLOWED, leave every digest as it was, and the check fails.

The verifier also recomputes the record's own digest and reports that it no longer matches the contents it is supposed to describe.

---- step 4: tamper with the record and verify again ----

Step 05the seam, and the rail

Run an executor that asks before it acts.

The executor is the seam. It presents the action to the service immediately before the rail call, and calls the rail only when the service says the action presented is the action authorized.

Three attempts go through it: an altered action, the action as authorized, then the same action again. rail() is the one function an integrator replaces, and in this run it is ours, a stand-in that returns a transaction reference.

What these five steps do not establish.

Three things are true of the run above, and none of them is closed by it.

The actor is declared, not verified.

The public service runs with the agent register off, so the identity on a request is what the caller says it is rather than something a credential bound. The record downloaded in step 3 says so in its own words, and the boundary client says the same at the end of its run: this service runs the shipped default, which is the register off. Nothing on this page shows that the caller is the agent it claims to be.

The rail is ours.

The rail in step 5 is a function inside our reference executor, and the executor behind the sandbox page is the sandbox app itself. Honoring a refusal is the integrating party's side of the seam, and a page cannot show their code deciding. What the count in step 5 shows is what our executor did when the service refused: nothing reached the rail.

The record is one party's account.

The acknowledgment block in the record downloaded in step 3 reads acknowledged_unsigned, with the note that the executor recorded what it received but did not sign back. Until an executor counter-signs with its own key, the record is Humsana's account of the action and the decision, not a two-party record.
