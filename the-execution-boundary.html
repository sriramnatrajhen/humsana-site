The execution boundary.

Humsana sits at the point where a consequential action crosses from one authority context into another. It asks whether the authority still covers what is being executed, and refuses when it does not.

The refusal names the field, both values and the rule, and it spends nothing. Honoring that refusal is the executor's job, which is why the seam matters as much as the check.

It does not replace the identity system, the agent runtime or the executor.

The stack holds the actor, the tool, the rule, the approval and the request. Existing controls can govern this case, and they often do.

What the executor settles

Everything. Identity, authority, the request and the approval sit in one log, under one owner.

What has to be carried

Nothing. The authority context never leaves the system that issued it.

Identity travels, but the authority for this specific action does not necessarily arrive with it. The approval stays in the system that made it.

What the executor settles

Who the actor is. The authority behind the action is asserted by the calling system, not shown.

What has to be carried

The approval itself, or the executor is asked to trust that it exists somewhere.

This is where the problem appears. The executor holds the request and almost nothing else: not the principal, not the delegation, not the approval behind it.

What the executor settles

That a request arrived. The counterparty's identity, policy and logs are not inspectable.

What has to be carried

The authority, the binding and the evidence, or the action is taken on trust.

Six stages carry authority to execution.

Each stage can be held, checked or lost. The boundary's job is the fifth one: to know what arrives at the executor and whether it is still covered.

Where the check has to sit.

The integrating party places the check on the path that acts, on its own side of the irreversible call. A refusal there is cheaper than reconstructing what happened afterwards.
